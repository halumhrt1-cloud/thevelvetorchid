There is a unique joy in bringing an orchid home. With their delicate, sculptured blooms and elegant stems, orchids instantly elevate any cozy room. However, many plant lovers find themselves dismayed when those stunning blooms drop and the plant begins to struggle.

Because orchids grow differently from typical houseplants, standard care routines often do more harm than good. The good news? Orchids are surprisingly resilient once you understand what they are trying to tell you. Here are five of the most common orchid mistakes and exactly how to fix them so your plants can thrive for years to come.

## 1. Watering with Ice Cubes (and Overwatering)

One of the most persistent myths in houseplant care is that watering an orchid with three ice cubes a week is ideal. In their native tropical habitats, most orchids grow attached to tree branches, receiving warm rain showers before drying out quickly in the gentle breeze. Freezing cold ice cubes can shock the tropical roots and lead to tissue damage.

Additionally, overwatering is the leading cause of orchid decline. When roots sit in stagnant water, they suffocate and turn into mushy brown threads.

### How to Fix It:
Ditch the ice cubes and adopt the soak-and-drain method. Water your orchid thoroughly with room-temperature water, letting it flow through the bark and out of the drainage holes. Allow the potting medium to dry out almost completely before watering again.

**Pro Tip:** Look at the roots through a clear pot. Plump, bright green roots mean the plant is hydrated. Silver or greyish roots indicate it is time for a drink.

## 2. Using Standard Potting Soil

Treating an orchid like a pothos or fiddle-leaf fig by planting it in heavy potting soil is a quick recipe for root rot. Most cultivated orchids, such as *Phalaenopsis*, are epiphytes. Their thick aerial roots require continuous airflow to stay healthy. Traditional soil smothers the roots and holds onto moisture for far too long.

### How to Fix It:
Always pot your orchids in a chunky, quick-draining orchid mix. A blend of coarse fir bark, perlite, and a little charcoal creates the ideal environment, allowing air to circulate freely around the root system. If you live in a very dry climate, adding a small amount of sphagnum moss can help retain just enough moisture without suffocating the roots.

## 3. Getting the Lighting Wrong

Finding the right spot for your orchid can feel like a balancing act. Too little light results in dark green, limp leaves and a refusal to rebloom. On the flip side, harsh, direct sunlight will scorch the fleshy leaves, leaving bleached spots or dark, burnt patches that never fade.

### How to Fix It:
Orchids love bright, indirect light. An east-facing windowsill that gets gentle morning sun is often the perfect spot. If your plant is near a bright south- or west-facing window, filter the light with a sheer linen curtain. Healthy leaves should be a light, vibrant apple-green color.

## 4. Letting Water Pool in the Leaf Crown

When watering from above, it is easy for water droplets to settle in the center crease of the leaves, known as the crown. If water sits in the crown for extended periods—especially in cooler indoor temperatures—it creates an ideal environment for crown rot. Crown rot destroys the core of the plant, often causing the leaves to drop off one by one until the plant cannot be saved.

### How to Fix It:
Always direct your watering can at the root base, beneath the leaves. If you accidentally get water into the center crown or between leaf joints, gently dab it dry with a clean paper towel or cotton swab. Good air circulation in your room also helps moisture evaporate safely.

## 5. Cutting Green Flower Spikes Too Early

Once an orchid finishes blooming, it is tempting to snip off the long stem right away. However, if the spike remains green and healthy, cutting it back completely deprives you of potential secondary blooms.

### How to Fix It:
Only trim a spike down to the base of the plant if it has turned completely brown, dry, and woody. If the stem remains green after the flowers drop, look closely for small bumps along the stem (called nodes). Cut the spike about half an inch above the second or third node from the bottom. Quite often, a secondary branch will shoot out from a node and reward you with a second display of flowers!

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are my orchid leaves wrinkled and leathery?
Wrinkled, soft leaves are a sign of dehydration. This happens either because the plant is not getting enough water, or because the roots have rotted from overwatering and can no longer absorb moisture. Check the roots first: if they are dry and silvery, give the plant a good soak; if they are dark and mushy, trim the rotted roots and repot immediately.

### How often should I fertilize my orchid?
Orchids are light feeders. Use a balanced, water-soluble orchid fertilizer diluted to one-quarter strength every two to three weeks during active growth in spring and summer. Follow the golden rule: "weakly, weekly." Pause fertilizing entirely while the plant is in peak bloom or resting during winter.

### When is the best time to repot an orchid?
Repot your orchid every one to two years, ideally right after it finishes blooming and fresh new root tips or leaves begin to grow. Avoid repotting while the plant is in full flower, as the stress can cause the blossoms to drop prematurely.
